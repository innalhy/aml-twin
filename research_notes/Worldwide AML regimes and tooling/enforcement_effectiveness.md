# Global AML enforcement cases and evidence on AML effectiveness (as of October 2026)

Scope note: Compiled 13 October 2026 from web search and fetch results. Wherever possible, figures come from regulator or DOJ pages. Where only secondary or vendor sources were found, the source is labelled. Older facts carry their year. Industry-sponsored estimates (LexisNexis/Forrester, vendor blogs) are flagged as such and kept apart from independent or academic research.

## Landmark bank AML failures: fine, year, and concrete control failures

### Takeaway
Across the big cases, the root causes repeat. Monitoring left out whole data feeds or products (TD's ACH and cheques, Metro's same-day transactions, Starling's partial sanctions list). Alerts and KYC reviews piled up faster than staff could clear them (ABN AMRO, Commerzbank, Cash App). Budget or growth outran controls (TD's "flat cost paradigm", Monzo and Starling's hypergrowth). Front-line staff were bribed or overridden (TD, EagleBank). Warnings from junior staff or whistleblowers went unheeded (Metro, Wirecard). These are process failures a simulation can model directly. They are not exotic typologies.

### Cited Findings

**TD Bank (US, 10 October 2024, ~US$3bn total, first US bank to plead guilty to money laundering conspiracy)**
- TD pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit money laundering. Penalties include about $1.8bn to DOJ and a record $1.3bn FinCEN penalty, about $3.09bn in total including other components. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/td-bank-pleads-guilty-money-laundering-case-will-pay-3-billion-penalti-rcna174912); [FinCEN](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-assesses-record-13-billion-penalty-against-td-bank); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/10/feds-slap-td-bank-with-3-1-billion-in-fines-for-money-laundering)
- About 92% of transaction volume, an estimated $18.3 trillion, went unmonitored between 1 January 2018 and 12 April 2024. The bank chose not to automatically monitor all domestic ACH and most cheque activity. — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/10/10/td-bank-money-laundering-charges-doj); [DOJ plea documents](https://www.justice.gov/media/1373151/dl)
- Senior executives enforced a "flat cost paradigm": the compliance budget could not grow year on year even as profits and risk rose. — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/10/feds-slap-td-bank-with-3-1-billion-in-fines-for-money-laundering); [Miami Business Law Review](https://business-law-review.law.miami.edu/?p=2342)
- Three laundering networks moved more than $670m through TD accounts between 2019 and 2023, per DOJ as reported by Fortune. The DOJ plea filing says "over $600 million" in criminal proceeds. One network moved more than $470m through large cash deposits into nominee accounts. — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/10/feds-slap-td-bank-with-3-1-billion-in-fines-for-money-laundering); [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/media/1373151/dl)
- Insider corruption: launderers bribed TD employees with about $57,000 in gift cards. Two TD employees were among roughly two dozen people prosecuted. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/td-bank-pleads-guilty-money-laundering-case-will-pay-3-billion-penalti-rcna174912)
- Remedies: a three-year independent monitor under the DOJ settlement, and an OCC asset cap barring TD's two US bank subsidiaries from exceeding $434bn in total assets. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/td-bank-pleads-guilty-money-laundering-case-will-pay-3-billion-penalti-rcna174912); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/10/feds-slap-td-bank-with-3-1-billion-in-fines-for-money-laundering)

**Danske Bank Estonia (Denmark/Estonia/US; December 2022 plea, ~US$2bn)**
- Danske pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit bank fraud, deceiving US correspondent banks, and agreed to forfeit about $2bn. About $850m paid to the SEC and Danish authorities was credited. The total was about $2.06bn: $1.21bn to the US government, $178.6m to the SEC, and a $672.3m criminal fine in Denmark. A US judge accepted the plea in January 2023. — [DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/danske-bank-pleads-guilty-fraud-us-banks-multi-billion-dollar-scheme-access-us); [The Local DK](https://www.thelocal.dk/20221214/danske-bank-admits-fraud-and-must-pay-2-billion-fine-in-us-case); [Business Insurance](https://www.businessinsurance.com/us-judge-accepts-danske-bank-guilty-plea-in-estonia-probe/)
- The Estonian branch's Non-Resident Portfolio (NRP) attracted customers by promising they could move large sums with little or no oversight. Employees helped hide beneficial owners through shell companies. About US$160bn of NRP transactions passed through US banks. Danske's own 2018 internal probe found about €200bn in payments through the Estonian branch. Sources give different periods: 2007–2015, 2008–2016, or 2008–2017. — [DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/danske-bank-pleads-guilty-fraud-us-banks-multi-billion-dollar-scheme-access-us); [ERR News](https://news.err.ee/1608819235/sports)

**HSBC (US, December 2012, US$1.921bn deferred prosecution agreement)**
- HSBC Holdings and HSBC Bank USA entered a DPA and paid $1.921bn in forfeiture and penalties, with five years under a monitor. At least $881m in drug-trafficking proceeds was processed, including for the Sinaloa Cartel (Mexico) and the Norte del Valle Cartel (Colombia). — [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2012/12/10/news/companies/hsbc-money-laundering/index.html); [HSBC 6-K (SEC)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001089113/000119312512499974/d451985dex992.htm)
- Between 2006 and 2009, HSBC Bank USA failed to monitor wire transfers ($670bn per CNN) and $9.4bn in cash transactions from HSBC Mexico. Wikipedia quotes "$670 million" for the wires instead. — [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2012/12/10/news/companies/hsbc-money-laundering/index.html); contradicted by [Wikipedia: HSBC México](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSBC_M%C3%A9xico)
- The ICIJ FinCEN Files later reported that HSBC kept moving large sums of suspect money after the 2012 settlement. — [ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/fincen-files/hsbc-moved-vast-sums-of-dirty-money-after-paying-record-laundering-fine/)

**Standard Chartered (US/UK, April 2019, ~US$1.1bn)**
- The OFAC settlement was $639m for 9,335 transactions worth about $437.5m processed through the US from June 2009 to May 2014. Most involved Iran-related accounts at the bank's UAE (Dubai) branches. A separate FCA fine of £102m covered AML and CFT control failures, including in the Middle East. A prior 2012 US settlement was $667m. In December 2025 the bank settled a £1.5bn investor suit alleging concealment. — [FinTech Futures](https://www.fintechfutures.com/regulatory-actions/standard-chartered-fined-1-1bn-for-bad-aml-and-sanctions-breach); [AML Intelligence](https://www.amlintelligence.com/2025/12/breaking-standard-chartered-settles-1-5bn-iran-sanctions-lawsuit/)

**ING (Netherlands, September 2018, €775m)**
- ING paid a €675m fine plus €100m disgorgement for breaches from 2010 to 2016. Accounts were used to launder hundreds of millions of euros. The bank failed to identify ultimate beneficial owners in some cases and missed unusual transactions. In 2024, 158 institutional investors sued for €500m over non-disclosure. — [Netherlands Public Prosecution Service](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2018/09/04/ing-pays-775-million-due-to-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention); [NL Times 2024](https://nltimes.nl/2024/02/08/investors-demand-eu500-mil-ing-disclosing-major-anti-money-laundering-case)

**ABN AMRO (Netherlands, April 2021, €480m)**
- ABN AMRO paid a €300m fine plus €180m disgorgement, the latter reflecting compliance costs the bank had saved, for the period 2014–2020. Failures covered client acceptance, transaction monitoring and client exit. The monitoring system produced more alerts than staff could handle, leaving a multi-year backlog. Three former board members were named as suspects. — [Netherlands Public Prosecution Service](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2021/04/19/abn-amro-pays-eur-480-million-on-account-of-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention); [ABN AMRO Q&A](https://www.abnamro.com/en/news/eight-questions-about-abn-amro-and-the-dutch-public-prosecution-services)
- A 2026 secondary report says DNB fined ABN AMRO €8.5m for failing to monitor its highest-risk customers adequately. This was not verified against a DNB release. — [Zyphe (vendor)](https://www.zyphe.com/resources/news/abn-amro-dnb-fine-customer-due-diligence-july-2026)

**Westpac (Australia, September 2020, A$1.3bn, record for Australia)**
- Westpac had more than 23 million contraventions, mostly failures to report International Funds Transfer Instructions (IFTIs) to AUSTRAC on time. Since 2013 it had known about child-exploitation risk in frequent low-value payments to the Philippines and South-East Asia, but it built no automated detection until 2018. In one case, a customer previously jailed for child exploitation opened several accounts. Westpac flagged one account and failed to review the others. — [ACFCS](https://www.acfcs.org/after-months-of-court-battles-westpac-settles-with-austrac-agrees-to-pay-1-3-billion-for-millions-of-aml-failings-ties-to-child-exploitation-network); [AML Intelligence](https://www.amlintelligence.com/2020/09/westpac-hit-with-record-billion-aus-dollar-fine-for-aml-breaches/); [Al Jazeera 2019](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2019/11/20/australias-westpac-accused-of-23-million-financial-breaches)
- Related precedent, CBA (2018, A$700m): 53,750 admitted contraventions. About 53,000 came from a 2012 software update ("coding error") that stopped intelligent deposit machines generating threshold transaction reports. — [AUSTRAC](https://www.austrac.gov.au/news-and-media/media-release/austrac-and-cba-agree-700m-penalty); [Business Standard/Reuters](https://www.business-standard.com/article/reuters/commonwealth-bank-says-coding-error-explains-alleged-money-laundering-breaches-117080700113_1.html)

**Deutsche Bank "mirror trades" (US/UK, January 2017, ~US$628m)**
- Penalties were $425m from NYDFS and £163m from the FCA, then the FCA's largest AML fine. About $10bn was moved out of Russia. Clients bought Russian blue chips in roubles in Moscow while related counterparties sold the same stock, in the same quantity and at the same price, through London for USD. The counterparties shared beneficial owners, management or agents. — [NYDFS](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/reports_and_publications/press_releases/pr1701301); [Global Investigations Review](https://globalinvestigationsreview.com/article/deutsche-bank-partly-resolves-mirror-trade-scandal-us628-million-settlement)

**Commerzbank London (UK, June 2020, £37.8m)**
- The fine covered failures from October 2012 to September 2017, with £54.0m before discount. By February 2017, 2,226 existing clients were overdue for KYC refresh. Other sources give about 2,000 or 2,350. An exceptions process was misused so the bank could keep dealing with clients whose checks were overdue. — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-commerzbank-london-37805400-over-anti-money-laundering-failures); [ICA](https://www.int-comp.org/insight/commerzbank-s-fca-fine-demonstrates-danger-of-aml-lapses/)

**Metro Bank (UK, November 2024, £16.7m)**
- More than 60 million transactions worth over £51bn went unmonitored. Because of a data-feed error, transactions on the day an account was opened, and any later ones until the account record updated, were never sent to monitoring. Junior staff raised concerns in 2017 and 2018, but the issue was not fixed. The fine was £23.8m before the 30% discount. — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-metro-bank-16m-financial-crime-failings); [AccountingWEB](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/business/finance-strategy/metro-bank-fined-ps167m-for-aml-failures)

**Starling Bank (UK, October 2024, £28.96m)**
- Since 2017, automated sanctions screening had checked customers against only "a fraction" of the full sanctions list. Starling found this in January 2023. After a 2021 FCA review it accepted a voluntary requirement (VREQ) not to onboard high-risk customers, then opened more than 54,000 accounts for about 49,000 high-risk customers between September 2021 and November 2023. FCA enforcement director Therese Chambers called the screening controls "shockingly lax". The fine was about £41m before discount. — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-starling-bank-failings-financial-crime-systems-and-controls); [FinTech Futures](https://www.fintechfutures.com/regulatory-actions/fca-fines-starling-bank-29m-for-sanctions-screening-failures)

**Monzo (UK, Final Notice 7 July 2025, £21.09m)**
- From October 2018 to August 2020, Monzo had inadequate onboarding, customer risk assessment, EDD, PEP handling and transaction monitoring while its customer base grew from about 600,000 (2018) to 5.8 million (2022). It then repeatedly breached a VREQ against onboarding high-risk customers (August 2020 to June 2022). A vendor analysis puts the breach at 33,039 accounts, 26,325 for high-risk customers. The fine was £30.1m before discount and included a £10m deterrence uplift for the repeated VREQ breaches. The VREQ was lifted in February 2025. — [Linklaters](https://financialregulation.linklaters.com/post/102ksp6/fca-fines-monzo-21m-for-failing-to-ensure-its-financial-crime-systems-and-contro); [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://connections.nortonrosefulbright.com/post/102kv0z/notice-in-a-nutshell-bank-fined-for-financial-crime-failings); [Zyphe (vendor)](https://www.zyphe.com/resources/blog/monzo-aml-fine-lessons)

**Credit Suisse, Mozambique "tuna bonds" (US/UK/Switzerland, October 2021, ~US$475m)**
- Fraud and FCPA charges covered two bond offerings and a syndicated loan to Mozambican state entities. The resolution included a guilty plea to wire-fraud conspiracy, $100m to the SEC, $200.6m to the FCA, and $200m of Mozambique's debt forgiven. — [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2021-213); [African Business](https://african.business/2021/10/finance-services/credit-suisse-fined-475m-over-tuna-bonds-scandal); [Spotlight on Corruption](https://www.spotlightcorruption.org/mozambique-and-the-tuna-bond-scandal/)

**Singapore S$3bn case (2023; MAS penalties 4 July 2025)**
- MAS imposed S$27.45m in composition penalties on nine financial institutions, its second-largest AML/CFT action after 1MDB (S$29.1m). Credit Suisse received the largest penalty (S$5.8m), and UOB and UBS were among the others. MAS found no pervasive lack of controls but "poor or inconsistent implementation". Five of the nine failed to do adequate customer risk assessments. Four individuals were banned. — [AsiaOne](https://www.asiaone.com/singapore/mas-issues-2745m-penalties-9-financial-institutions-over-3b-money-laundering-case); [The Online Citizen](https://www.theonlinecitizen.com/2025/07/04/mas-fines-9-financial-institutions-s27-45-million-over-s3-billion-money%E2%80%91laundering-case)

**Raiffeisen Bank International / Russia sanctions (2023–2025)**
- OFAC asked RBI in January 2023 to clarify its payments business in light of Russia/Ukraine. In 2024, Treasury sanctioned Russian companies used to disguise ownership of a $1.6bn industrial stake tied to RBI's aborted deal. No formal OFAC penalty against RBI was found. In October 2025 RBI was at the centre of EU sanctions-package debate. — [GlobalSanctions](https://globalsanctions.com/2023/02/ofac-investigating-raiffeisen-bank-international/); [AML Intelligence](https://www.amlintelligence.com/2024/05/latest-us-imposes-sanctions-after-aborted-sale-of-russian-tycoon-stake-to-raiffeisen-bank/); [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2025/10/14/austrian-bank-takes-centre-stage-as-eu-pushes-new-sanctions-on-russia)

**Wirecard (Germany, insolvency 25 June 2020)**
- €1.9bn in supposed trustee cash at two Philippine banks "likely did not exist", about a quarter of the balance sheet. BaFin investigated the FT journalists and short-sellers instead of the company, including filing a criminal complaint against two FT reporters, and imposed a short-selling ban. BaFin's president called the episode a "total disaster". The case is mainly an accounting fraud and supervisory failure, not a fine. — [Wikipedia: Wirecard scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirecard_scandal); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/06/29/enron-of-germany-wirecard-scandal-casts-a-shadow-on-governance.html); [LUISS working paper](https://leap.luiss.it/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/WP5.21-The-Wirecard-scandal-and-the-role-of-Bafin.pdf)

**Recent 2025–2026 actions (context)**
- Block/Cash App (NYDFS, April 2025, $40m plus monitor): an alert backlog left unaddressed and delayed SARs. Canaccord Genuity (FinCEN, 6 March 2026, $80m, the largest BSA penalty against a broker-dealer): an under-resourced AML program. EagleBank (DOJ non-prosecution agreement, 30 June 2026, ~$9.7m): senior executives allegedly overrode compliance staff's attempts to close cheque-kiting accounts. — [ComplyAdvantage (vendor)](https://complyadvantage.com/insights/the-biggest-aml-fines-in-2025/); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/mid-year-developments-in-anti-money-laundering-in-2026/)
- In 2026 the US policy direction is deregulatory. FinCEN's 7 April 2026 proposed AML program rule promotes risk-based programs, "does not contemplate regulatory second-guessing" of reasonable resource decisions, and limits maintenance-based enforcement to "significant or systemic" failures. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/mid-year-developments-in-anti-money-laundering-in-2026/)

### Inferences
- The most reusable failure patterns for a simulation are these. (1) Monitoring coverage gaps: products or data feeds silently excluded (TD, Metro, Starling, CBA). (2) Backlogs: alerts or KYC refreshes outrunning capacity (ABN AMRO, Commerzbank, Cash App). (3) Growth outrunning controls (Monzo, Starling, TD's flat budget). (4) Override or corruption of front-line decisions (TD gift cards, EagleBank executives). (5) Correspondent or non-resident flows treated as low risk (Danske NRP, HSBC Mexico).
- Coverage gaps are invisible to an investigator who only sees alerts. A trainee should learn to ask what is not generating alerts, not just clear the queue.
- Several cases show escalations that did not land (Metro's junior staff, Wirecard whistleblowers, EagleBank compliance staff). An append-only event log of escalations and overrides addresses exactly this failure.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch primary DOJ, OCC or Fed text for TD (the DOJ press page returned empty). The OCC's separate penalty amount and any 2025–2026 monitorship status are unverified.
- The HSBC wire-monitoring figure ($670bn vs $670m) is unresolved. Check the DOJ 2012 DPA statement of facts or the 2012 US Senate PSI report. Commonly cited HSBC details, such as Mexico rated lowest-risk and large alert backlogs, were not confirmed in this session.
- The Bruun & Hjejle Danske report (2018) was not retrieved, so its specific findings (alert handling, the Estonian IT platform not integrated with group monitoring) are uncited.
- No 2025–2026 AUSTRAC or OCC enforcement data was retrieved. The Entain/AUSTRAC and Mounties matters were not checked.
- Whether OFAC has ever penalised RBI is unconfirmed.

## Evidence on AML effectiveness: interdiction, SAR conversion, FATF ratings, academic critique

### Takeaway
Independent researchers converge on a low figure. Roughly 0.1–1% of estimated criminal proceeds is ever confiscated: Pol puts AML's impact at about 0.1% or less, and Europol's EU data found about 1.1% finally confiscated. The FATF's own 2022 stocktake found most assessed countries at low or moderate effectiveness, with only 19% achieving high or substantial effectiveness on confiscation. All of these figures rest on weak denominators, and the authors say so themselves.

### Cited Findings
- Ronald Pol, "Anti-money laundering: The world's least effective policy experiment? Together, we can fix it", *Policy Design and Practice* 3(1), 2020, pp. 73–94. He concludes AML's impact on criminal finances is about 0.1% or less, and cautions that the underlying data are poorly validated and methodologically inconsistent. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/25741292.2020.1725366); [cited in arXiv 2510.09840](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09840)
- Pol's earlier work (Journal of Financial Crime, 2018) used seizure and forfeiture rates as an effectiveness proxy and called the model "almost completely ineffective". RNZ's 2018 coverage reports global recovery of about 0.2% of an estimated $2trn of criminal funds a year, and about 1% for New Zealand. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eme/jfcpps/jfc-08-2017-0071.html); [RNZ 2018](https://www.radionz.co.nz/national/programmes/morningreport/audio/2018647115/anti-money-laundering-rules-ineffective-study)
- Europol, "Does crime still pay?" (2016, covering 2010–2014 data): 2.2% of estimated criminal proceeds in the EU were provisionally seized or frozen and 1.1% finally confiscated, so 98.9% remain with criminals. The base estimate is about €110bn a year, within a range of €92bn to €188bn. Critics argue that comparing seized assets with gross criminal income is methodologically flawed. — [Europol](https://www.europol.europa.eu/newsroom/news/does-crime-still-pay); [Tristram Hicks commentary](https://tristramhicks.substack.com/p/does-crime-pay-in-the-european-union)
- FATF 2022 *Report on the State of Effectiveness and Compliance*, covering about 120 assessed countries: only 19% showed high or substantial effectiveness in confiscating criminal proceeds. FATF itself notes widespread "tick-box" adoption of laws without a focus on results. A search snippet suggests 97% of assessed countries have low-to-moderate ratings on some outcome, but which outcome was not confirmed. — [FATF report](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/reports/Report-on-the-State-of-Effectiveness-Compliance-with-FATF-Standards.pdf.coredownload.pdf); [RUSI](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/policy-briefs/boosting-fatfs-global-asset-recovery-responses); [i-AML summary](https://i-aml.com/financial-crime/fatf-2022-report-on-state-of-effectiveness-and-compliance-with-fatf-standards/)
- FATF rates effectiveness on 11 Immediate Outcomes (High, Substantial, Moderate or Low). A country is referred to the ICRG ("grey list" review) if 9 or more outcomes are rated low or moderate with at least 2 low, or if 6 or more are rated low. — [FATF procedures](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/methodology/Assessment-Follow-Up-ICRG-Procedures-2022.pdf.coredownload.inline.pdf)
- Halliday, Levi and Reuter, *Global Surveillance of Dirty Money* (2014). FATF objectives were too vague to evaluate. IMF/FATF assessments measured formal compliance, not outcomes. There is no credible evidence that compliance reduces crime or proceeds. The regime ignores the costs and "bads" it creates. — [Cardiff ORCA PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/88168/1/Report_Global%20Surveillance%20of%20Dirty%20Money%201.30.2014.pdf); [Levi, Reuter & Halliday, Asian J. Criminology 2018/2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11417-020-09319-y)
- UK SAR conversion, per older research cited by RUSI: about 89.8% of SARs get initial checks, 66.8% are fully examined, and only about 6.3% prompt further investigation or law-enforcement action. The piece is from about 2015. — [RUSI](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/suspicious-activity-reports-regime-information-sharing-heart-tackling-financial-crime)
- UK SAR volume: 634,000+ SARs in the NCA's 2017 annual report, with £56.5m in assets denied. The UKFIU received 901,255 in 2021–22 and about 859,000 in 2022–23. Lack of feedback to reporters drives "defensive filing". — [NCA 2017](https://www.nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk/news/suspicious-activity-reports-regime-annual-report-2017-published); [Macfarlanes](https://www.macfarlanes.com/insights/102j4d8/suspicious-activity-reports-signs-of-quality-over-quantity/)

### Inferences
- Effectiveness is lost mostly downstream of the bank (FIU triage, investigation, confiscation). But the inputs banks control, SAR quality and timeliness, determine whether the roughly 6% that get actioned are the right 6%. A simulation should score narrative usefulness, not filing volume.
- The 0.1% and 1% figures measure different things (Pol's policy impact vs Europol's confiscation ratio). A report should not present them as interchangeable.

### Gaps
- No post-2022 FATF aggregate effectiveness statistics were retrieved, and the fifth round started in 2024–25.
- No current US figure on the share of SARs used by law enforcement was retrieved. FinCEN publishes "value of BSA data" anecdotes, not conversion rates.
- Transparency International commentary was not retrieved in this session.
- No recent UKFIU annual figures (2023–24 or 2024–25) were retrieved.

## Compliance costs, false positives and alert backlogs

### Takeaway
The headline cost figures come from industry-sponsored studies: LexisNexis Risk Solutions, conducted by Forrester. They put global financial-crime compliance at US$206.1bn (2023) and US/Canada at $61bn (2024). The widely quoted 90–95% false-positive rate is an industry estimate with weak provenance, not a measured independent statistic.

### Cited Findings
- LexisNexis Risk Solutions, *True Cost of Financial Crime Compliance* global study (September 2023; **industry-sponsored**): US$206.1bn worldwide, from a survey of 1,181 compliance professionals. Nearly all institutions reported rising costs, and 72% use analytics or AI in compliance. — [LexisNexis press release](https://risk.lexisnexis.com/global/en/about-us/press-room/press-release/20230926-global-financial-crime-compliance-costs)
- LexisNexis/Forrester US & Canada study (February 2024; **industry-sponsored**): $61bn a year, with costs up for 99% of institutions and 70% prioritising cost cutting. Earlier editions put the US/Canada figure at $49.9bn (2021) and $56.7bn (2022). — [LexisNexis 2024](https://risk.lexisnexis.com/global/en/about-us/press-room/press-release/20240221-true-cost-of-compliance-us-ca); [LexisNexis 2022](https://risk.lexisnexis.com/about-us/press-room/press-release/20220929-report-reveals-the-yearly-cost-of-financial-crime-compliance)
- The false-positive rate is widely cited as about 95% of transaction-monitoring alerts. Sources trace it variously to PwC or a 2020 Global Investigations Review piece. Definitions differ: some count all machine alerts, others only analyst-queue alerts. — [The Stack](https://www.thestack.technology/transaction-monitoring-banking/); [FluxForce (vendor)](https://www.fluxforce.ai/statistics/false-positive-rates-transaction-monitoring)
- ABN AMRO's alert volume exceeded staff capacity and produced a multi-year backlog. Commerzbank had about 2,226 overdue KYC refreshes. Cash App was fined in part for an unaddressed alert backlog. — see case citations above: [OM](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2021/04/19/abn-amro-pays-eur-480-million-on-account-of-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention); [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-commerzbank-london-37805400-over-anti-money-laundering-failures); [ComplyAdvantage](https://complyadvantage.com/insights/the-biggest-aml-fines-in-2025/)
- The ABN AMRO settlement's €180m disgorgement explicitly priced in compliance costs saved, a regulator's acknowledgement that under-investment is a gain. — [OM](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2021/04/19/abn-amro-pays-eur-480-million-on-account-of-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention)

### Inferences
- Put the roughly $206bn a year in costs (industry estimate) next to the roughly 1% confiscation rate (Europol) and the cost-benefit gap is the core of the academic critique. Both numbers are soft.
- A training simulation should make alert volume and the false-positive ratio a visible constraint. Trainees should feel the trade-off between handling time and quality that produced the ABN AMRO and Cash App backlogs.

### Gaps
- No LexisNexis global compliance edition newer than 2023, or US/Canada edition newer than February 2024, was found. A 2025 LexisNexis release located was the separate *True Cost of Fraud* study.
- No independent, non-vendor academic measurement of false-positive rates was found.
- No reliable global headcount figure for AML/compliance staff was found.

## Side effects: de-risking, financial exclusion and "debanking"

### Takeaway
FATF now says in its own guidance that wholesale de-risking contradicts the risk-based approach. It revised Recommendation 1 in February 2025 and issued new financial-inclusion guidance in mid-2025. Meanwhile "debanking" has become politicised: the Farage/Coutts affair in the UK (2023) and the US executive order of August 2025, followed by 2026 DOJ subpoenas.

### Cited Findings
- February 2025: FATF revised Recommendation 1 and its Interpretive Note to address unintended consequences, including de-risking and exclusion. The new Financial Inclusion guidance (dated June 2025; some sources say July) states that "Wholesale de-risking is contrary to the RBA and contributes to broader financial exclusion". It supports simplified measures in low-risk cases and is non-binding. — [FATF guidance PDF](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/content/dam/fatf-gafi/guidance/Guidance-Financial-Inclusion%20-Anti-Money-Laundering-Terrorist-Financing-Measures.pdf.coredownload.pdf); [Global AML/CFT EU](https://www.global-amlcft.eu/financial-inclusion-fatf-guidance/)
- CGAP welcomed the guidance but criticised it for addressing de-risking while ignoring rule-based measures that drive exclusion. The civil-society FATF Platform tracked the June 2025 plenary outcomes for NPOs. — [CGAP](https://www.cgap.org/blog/fatfs-revised-inclusion-guidance-progress-made-questions-remain); [FATF Platform](https://fatfplatform.org/news/key-outcomes-from-the-june-2025-fatf-plenary-impacting-civil-society/)
- US Executive Order 14331, "Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans" (7 August 2025), directs regulators to end "politicized or unlawful" debanking, remove reputational risk from supervisory materials, and require "individualized, objective, and risk-based analyses". It set a 120-day review. — [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/president-trump-signs-debanking-executive-order); [Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/08/president-trump-signs-fair-banking-executive-order-directing-financial-regulators-to-remedy)
- On 10 June 2026 the WSJ reported that the US Attorney's Office for DC had subpoenaed several large banks for names of debanked individuals and closure reasons, with possible FIRREA theories. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/mid-year-developments-in-anti-money-laundering-in-2026/)
- UK: Coutts/NatWest closed Nigel Farage's accounts in July 2023, and the CEOs of Coutts and NatWest resigned. In a review of 34 firms, the FCA found none closed accounts "primarily because of" political views, and financial crime concerns were the most common reason. The FCA also launched a PEP-regime review. The IEA argues banks exit customers when gathering evidence costs too much, not when evidence proves criminality. — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-09-19/fca-finds-no-evidence-banks-rejected-politicians-over-views); [IEA](https://iea.org.uk/publications/debanked-the-economic-and-social-consequences-of-anti-money-laundering-regulation/)
- Halliday, Levi and Reuter note that the FATF system historically ignored the "bads" it produces. — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/88168/1/Report_Global%20Surveillance%20of%20Dirty%20Money%201.30.2014.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a simulation, "exit the customer" should be an option with a recorded cost (exclusion and displacement of risk), not a free safe choice. Under the 2025 FATF guidance and the US EO, an exit needs an individualised, documented rationale.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data was retrieved on correspondent-banking decline (BIS/CPMI tracking), MSB or NGO account closures, small island states (e.g. Pacific/Caribbean), or refugee access in this session.
- The outcomes of the US 120-day regulator review and the FCA PEP review were not retrieved.

## Shifts in typologies 2024–2026

### Takeaway
The enforcement and advisory focus has moved towards Chinese money laundering networks serving Mexican cartels, scam and APP-fraud laundering through mule accounts, and sanctions evasion. In the UK, liability now sits partly with the receiving bank, which raises the stakes of mule detection.

### Cited Findings
- On 28 August 2025, FinCEN issued Advisory FIN-2025-A003 and a Financial Trend Analysis on Chinese money laundering networks (CMLNs). It analysed more than 137,000 BSA reports covering about $312bn in suspicious activity (January 2020 to December 2024). CMLNs serve Mexico-based cartels, including the Sinaloa Cartel and CJNG (designated FTOs), and profit by selling cartel dollars to Chinese nationals evading China's roughly $50,000 annual FX limit. Red flags include students, retirees and homemakers with unexplained wealth. — [Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0231); [Thompson Hine](https://www.thompsonhinesmartrade.com/2025/09/fincen-issues-advisory-and-financial-trend-analysis-on-chinese-money-laundering-networks/); [AP via News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/business/2025/08/28/the-treasury-department-wants-us-banks-to-monitor-for-suspected-chinese-money-laundering-networks/)
- UK APP scam reimbursement became mandatory from 7 October 2024 for Faster Payments and CHAPS. It is capped at £85,000 per claim, with the cost split 50/50 between sending and receiving PSPs. Reimbursement is due within five business days, and claims can be refused after 13 months. — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/briefings/2024/09/authorised-push-payment-fraud-a-new-mandatory-reimbursement-regime-for-uk-psps); [PSR PS25/5](https://www.psr.org.uk/media/rhelv4op/ps25-5-app-scams-reimbursement-consolidated-policy-statement-may-2025.pdf)
- A secondary source reports that 88% of money lost to in-scope APP fraud (about £112m) was returned between 7 October 2024 and 30 June 2025. Unverified against the PSR. — [Edgar Dunn](https://www.edgardunn.com/articles/one-year-on-uks-app-fraud-reimbursement-rules)
- Sanctions evasion: Deutsche's mirror trades (Russia), Standard Chartered's Dubai branch Iran flows, Starling's partial-list screening and the RBI Russia scrutiny show sanctions failures sitting alongside AML failures. — see case citations above
- Professional enablers and shell companies: Danske employees helped conceal owners through shell companies, and ING failed to identify UBOs. — [DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/danske-bank-pleads-guilty-fraud-us-banks-multi-billion-dollar-scheme-access-us); [OM](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2018/09/04/ing-pays-775-million-due-to-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention)
- TD shows the mule and nominee pattern: large cash deposits into nominee accounts, $470m+ through one network. — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/10/feds-slap-td-bank-with-3-1-billion-in-fines-for-money-laundering)

### Inferences
- The 50/50 sending and receiving split in the UK makes inbound-payment (mule account) monitoring a direct cost line for the receiving bank. That is a good simulation branch: a trainee at the receiving bank decides whether to freeze or return funds.

### Gaps
- No trade-based money laundering (TBML) data for 2024–2026 was retrieved, beyond the FATF and FinCEN framing in general terms.
- No current UK mule-account statistics (Cifas / UK Finance) were retrieved.
- No exact SAR key term for CMLN filings was confirmed from the FinCEN advisory text.

## Implications for training AML investigators

### Takeaway
The enforcement record says investigators fail less from missing exotic typologies than from coverage blind spots, backlogs, weak documentation and overridden escalations. A training twin should score completeness of coverage, timeliness, narrative quality and an auditable decision trail. Speed alone is the wrong measure.

### Cited Findings
- Backlogs are themselves an enforcement finding: ABN AMRO, Commerzbank, Cash App. — [OM](https://www.prosecutionservice.nl/latest/news/2021/04/19/abn-amro-pays-eur-480-million-on-account-of-serious-shortcomings-in-money-laundering-prevention); [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-commerzbank-london-37805400-over-anti-money-laundering-failures)
- Misused exceptions processes and executive overrides are cited as aggravating factors. Commerzbank used exceptions to keep transacting with overdue-KYC clients, and EagleBank executives overrode compliance staff. — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-commerzbank-london-37805400-over-anti-money-laundering-failures); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/mid-year-developments-in-anti-money-laundering-in-2026/)
- Unheeded escalation: Metro's junior staff flagged unmonitored data in 2017 and 2018 without a fix. — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-metro-bank-16m-financial-crime-failings)
- Partial-pattern review: Westpac flagged one account of a known child-exploitation offender and did not review linked accounts. — [ACFCS](https://www.acfcs.org/after-months-of-court-battles-westpac-settles-with-austrac-agrees-to-pay-1-3-billion-for-millions-of-aml-failings-ties-to-child-exploitation-network)
- Defensive filing and a lack of feedback degrade SAR value, and only about 6% of UK SARs led to action in older RUSI-cited data. — [RUSI](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/suspicious-activity-reports-regime-information-sharing-heart-tackling-financial-crime)
- The US direction from 2025 to 2026 requires individualised, documented risk decisions for exits (EO 14331) and avoids second-guessing reasonable risk-based resource allocation (FinCEN April 2026 proposal). Either way, the documented rationale is what gets examined. — [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/president-trump-signs-debanking-executive-order); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/mid-year-developments-in-anti-money-laundering-in-2026/)

### Inferences
These are design suggestions, not sourced facts.
- **Coverage questions:** include scenarios where the alert queue is clean but a feed is missing (Metro, TD, Starling). Reward the trainee for asking for data lineage or noticing that same-day activity is absent.
- **Link analysis discipline:** when one account is flagged, the expected action is to review all related accounts and parties (Westpac, TD nominee accounts).
- **Backlog pressure:** model a queue with roughly 90%+ false positives and a service-level clock. Score both handling time and the miss rate on true positives so trainees see the ABN AMRO trade-off.
- **SAR narrative quality:** score who/what/when/where/why/how, linked parties and typology tag (e.g. CMLN key terms), not just whether a SAR was filed. This counters defensive filing.
- **Escalation and override as events:** every escalation, exception and senior override is logged as an immutable event with rationale and approver. This matches the repo's rules-before-AI and "human override stored as event" contract in AGENTS.md, and mirrors the Commerzbank, EagleBank and Metro failures.
- **Exit decisions carry cost:** a "de-risk / exit" choice should record rationale and an exclusion side effect, reflecting the 2025 FATF guidance and EO 14331.
- **Restriction compliance:** a scenario where the institution is under a VREQ (Starling, Monzo) or asset cap (TD), and the trainee must refuse onboarding that the business line pushes.

### Gaps
- No empirical studies were found on investigator training effectiveness or on how alert-handling time correlates with SAR quality.
- No regulator-published benchmarks were found for acceptable alert ageing or handling time. These would be needed to calibrate a simulation's service-level parameters.
